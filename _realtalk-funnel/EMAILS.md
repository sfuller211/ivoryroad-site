## Email 2 — Day 2
**Subject:** What "fine" usually means
**Preview text:** It is rarely the whole answer

You ask how his day was.

Fine.

You ask if something happened.

Nope.

And that is the end of it, because pushing feels like prying and letting it go feels like giving up.

Here is what I have come to believe about that word. "Fine" is not usually a door closing. It is usually a boy who has not yet found language for something, being asked to produce it on demand, in a hallway, while taking his shoes off.

He is not refusing. He is not ready.

The move that works better is not a better question. It is a worse moment. Side by side instead of face to face. Driving, dishes, walking the dog, late at night when the lights are off. Somewhere he does not have to look at you.

Try this: say nothing for the first ten minutes of the drive. Most parents cannot stand it. Teen guys often start talking somewhere in minute eleven.

Scott

## Email 3 — Day 4
**Subject:** After he blows up
**Preview text:** The 20 minutes that matter most

Something small sets him off. The tone, the door, the thing he says that he does not mean.

And then it passes, and you are both standing in the wreckage of an argument about a phone charger.

The instinct is to address it right then. To get an apology, or an explanation, or at minimum an acknowledgment that this was not okay.

He usually cannot give you that yet. Not because he is stubborn. Because he can get to sorry a long time before he can get to why.

So the twenty minutes after are worth more than the argument itself. Not a lecture. Not a debrief. Just proof that the relationship survived it.

Something like: "That got big. I am still here. We can talk later or not at all."

Then leave. Actually leave.

The repair matters more than the ruling. And if you make the repair reliable enough, the why shows up on its own, usually days later, usually when you are doing something else.

Scott

## Email 4 — Day 7
**Subject:** Why I wrote Real Talk
**Preview text:** It started with the same silence

For a long time I watched teen guys carry things they had no words for, and watched the adults who loved them try to help with the only tools anyone had given them. More questions. More talks. More pressure disguised as concern.

It mostly did not work. Not because anyone was doing it wrong, but because we were asking boys to explain feelings they had never once been taught to name.

So I wrote the thing I wished existed.

**Real Talk: Becoming Who God Made You to Be** is written to the teen guy directly, not about him. It covers identity, emotions, and real faith, in his language, without a lecture. Twelve short chapters. He can read one in ten minutes.

There is a Parent Guide that runs alongside it, so you know what he is working through without having to ask him to summarize it. And a Small Group Leader Guide if your church or school wants to run it with a group.
