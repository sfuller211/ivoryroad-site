**Rules for every email in this sequence**
- The Amazon link points at `ivoryroad.shop/go/amazon/student/`, never directly at Amazon, so the click is countable.
- That redirect carries an Amazon Attribution tag. **Corrected Sep 25 2026:** an earlier note read "never an Associates tag, Amazon prohibits Special Links in email." That rule is real, but it belongs to the **Associates** affiliate programme and was wrongly applied here. Attribution is a separate measurement programme with no commission, and Amazon's own guide lists Email as one of its six supported channels. Do not strip the tag.
- The Conversation Cards link points at `ivoryroad.shop/go/cards/` for the same reason.
- `ivoryroad.shop` sits under the signature in every email. The guide itself carries it on all ten pages, so the emails match it. It is a signature line, never a second call to action.
- No em dashes.
- Recognition first. The ask comes last, or not at all.

**Voice rules — added Sep 25 2026**

1. **Use contractions.** The first version had almost none. "He is not refusing." "Most parents cannot stand it." Formal reads as written rather than said.
2. **One rhetorical turn per email, maximum.** The first version ran "not a door closing, it is a boy who" then "not refusing, not ready" then "not a better question, a worse moment" in four paragraphs. One is a good line. Four in a row is a writer performing.
3. **Never make the parent the obstacle.** "Somewhere he does not have to look at you" reads as blame. It is his own face he does not want watched. These readers already arrive feeling like they did something wrong, and the guide opens by telling them they are not failing. The emails must not undo that.
4. **Every email ends with one small thing to try this week**, the way each practice in the guide does. Recognition, then one action, then stop.

**On using their first name**

The form collects it and it is required, so `{$firstname}` is always populated. Use it in **Email 1 only**, the one asking for a reply. A name in all six is what makes automated mail feel automated. Emails 2 through 6 open cold on purpose.

## Email 2 — Day 2
### Practice 1 — Quiet Car Moments
**Subject:** What "fine" usually means
**Preview text:** It's rarely the whole answer

You ask how his day was.

Fine.

You ask if something happened.

Nope.

And that's the end of it, because pushing feels like prying and letting it go feels like giving up.

Most of the time "fine" isn't him shutting you out. He just doesn't have the words yet, and you're asking for them in the doorway while his shoes are half off.

He'd tell you if he could. Just not on the spot.

There's a reason the first practice in the guide is about the car. Side by side, no eye contact, engine noise doing half the work. It's easier to say a hard thing when nobody is watching your face.

**This week.** Pick one short drive. Before you start it, decide this ride is for a small connection and not a big talk. Turn the music down a little.

Then say nothing for the first ten minutes.

Most of us can't stand the quiet that long. A lot of teen guys start talking right around the ten minute mark.

If he doesn't, open with something specific instead of "how was your day." There's a page of these in the guide. "What was one not-terrible part of today?" works better than it should.

And if you get one word back, that's still a fine outcome. Thank him and move on. The point of the ride was the ride.

Scott

## Email 3 — Day 4
### Practice 2 — One Honest Question
**Subject:** Ask one thing, then stop
**Preview text:** The hard part isn't the question

Be strong. Be funny. Be chill. Be fine.

That's most of what a teenage guy hears about how to be, and none of it leaves room for whatever he's actually carrying. So he runs the version of himself that costs the least.

Underneath it, a lot of them are quietly wondering whether it's okay to say what they really feel.

You can answer that without ever asking it.

Ask one honest thing. Then do the hard part, which is nothing. Don't fix it. Don't match it with a story of your own. Don't turn it into a lesson while you happen to have his attention.

"Thank you for telling me that" is a complete response. So is "that makes sense."

**This week.** Pick a moment where you're side by side rather than across from each other. Dishes, a walk, shooting hoops. Ask one question and only one.

"What's been heavy for you lately?" is a good one. So is "on the inside, are you more tired, stressed, or okay?"

The wording matters less than you think. He isn't testing the question. He's testing what you do with the answer.

Scott

## Email 4 — Day 7
### Practice 3 — Safe Places to Feel
**Subject:** Why it comes out as anger
**Preview text:** Usually it's the only door that's open

Somewhere along the way he learned that big feelings are too much.

Nobody sat him down and told him. He picked it up from a locker room, a group chat, a movie, maybe from watching a grown man he respects go quiet instead of sad.

The feelings still come. They just come out the one door that feels safe.

Which is usually anger. Sometimes a joke. Sometimes a closed bedroom door.

Worth knowing when he snaps at you over nothing, because it means the snapping is rarely about you and almost never about the phone charger.

**This week.** Watch for one moment where something peeks through. His tone. His posture. Some comment he tosses out on his way past you.

Then name it instead of correcting it.

"It seems like that made you angry." "That sounds really disappointing." "I can tell that hurt."

Then one short line that costs you nothing. "I'm here with you in this." Or "you don't have to have it all figured out."

None of that lets him off the hook for how he behaves. Boundaries stay exactly where they were. You're only telling him he can feel the thing and still belong here.

Scott

## Email 5 — Day 10
### Practice 4 — Name the Good You See
**Subject:** Mostly a problem to be fixed
**Preview text:** Add up what he hears in a week

Add up what he hears from adults in an average week.

Grades. Chores. Attitude. Phone. Shoes in the hallway. Tone. Most of it is fair, none of it is cruel, and every piece of it is technically true.

But it stacks. And somewhere in the stack a lot of teenage guys quietly decide they're mostly a problem being managed.

The answer isn't praise. He can spot manufactured praise from across the room, and it makes the real stuff worth less.

It's noticing something true about his character and saying it out loud on a day he didn't earn anything.

**This week.** Watch for one small thing. He helped his brother without being asked. He showed up when he clearly didn't want to. He kept going when it got hard. He made someone laugh at exactly the right second.

Tell him inside a day. Keep it short and calm.

"You could've quit and you didn't. That takes something."

Then stop talking. Don't attach a correction to the end of it, even a small one, even a fair one. It cancels the whole thing.

He isn't a project you're managing. He's a person God is still shaping, and pointing at the evidence is one of the few things that reliably gets through.

Scott
