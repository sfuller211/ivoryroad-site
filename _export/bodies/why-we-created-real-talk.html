We were at dinner with my brother-in-law and sister-in-law when the conversation shifted.

She started talking about their son. He was 12 or 13 at the time, and she was describing things she was noticing in him. Things he was carrying. Things she didn't quite know how to reach.

I knew exactly what she was describing.

I'd been a junior high pastor. I'd sat across from boys in those years, watched them try to figure out who they were, watched faith either land or bounce off depending on whether anyone was willing to start with what they were actually feeling. I'd seen what that age can do to a kid and what it can do to a family.

And I'd been that kid once. I remembered what it was like to be a teenage boy trying to make sense of who I was, what God thought about me, and whether any of it connected to real life.

Sitting at that dinner table, I thought: what can I give him?

That question became Real Talk.

What I kept seeing across all three of those experiences

Whether I was the kid, the pastor, or the parent: the gap was the same.

Resources existed. Connection wasn't happening.

Most faith content for teen boys leads with inspiration or instruction. Big ideas. A call to be better. A call to be a leader. That approach has its place. But a lot of boys, especially the quiet ones who seem mostly fine, aren't looking for motivation. They're looking for someone to name what they're already feeling.

There's a real difference between content that teaches a boy what to believe and content that meets him where he actually is. The first kind often bounces off. The second kind makes him put the book down for a second because something landed.

Real Talk was built around that second kind.

What the book actually is

It's not a devotional. It's not a curriculum with a seven-step framework. It's a conversation.

One that starts by taking a boy's inner world seriously before offering him anything to do with it.

Each chapter opens with a relatable scenario. One a teen boy would recognize from his own life. A tension that doesn't get neatly resolved by page three. That's intentional. Boys who are practiced at tuning out faith content will sometimes stay longer when it doesn't feel like it's already rushing toward a conclusion before they've caught up.

The language is plain. The scenarios are honest. The faith isn't layered on top; it runs through the actual content.

We wanted something a parent could hand to their son without him immediately sensing that it was designed to fix him.

Who it's for

It's for your son. But it's also for you.

There's a parent guide for a reason. Because one of the hardest parts of raising a teenage boy is knowing when to say something and knowing what to say when you do. The parent guide gives you conversation starters, questions that actually open things up, and context for what your son is working through in each chapter.

Real Talk is for the boy who seems okay but isn't quite. The one who's present but not really engaged. The one who's working hard at being whatever he thinks he's supposed to be and getting quietly tired of it.

If that sounds like your son, even a little, this is what we made.

Real Talk is dedicated to every middle school student I had the honor of pastoring, and to my nephew Matthew, whose heart, humor, and honesty inspired these pages.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Real Talk?

Real Talk is a faith-based book series for teen boys and the parents and leaders who walk alongside them. It addresses identity, emotions, and real-life faith in plain, honest language that teen boys can actually sit with.

Who is Real Talk written for?

The books are written for teen boys ages 12-18 who are navigating questions about who they are, how they fit, and what God thinks about them. There is also a parent guide and a leader guide for the adults in their lives.

How is Real Talk different from other Christian books for teen boys?

Real Talk starts with a boy's actual experience before offering any answers or direction. Most faith content for teens leads with inspiration or instruction. Real Talk leads with recognition, naming what a teen boy is already feeling before asking anything of him.
