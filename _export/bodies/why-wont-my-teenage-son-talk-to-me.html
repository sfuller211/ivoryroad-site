He used to tell you everything. The whole play by play, who said what, what was funny, what wasn't. Now you get one word. Fine. Nothing. It was okay. Then the door closes, sometimes with a slam, more often with a quiet thud that somehow says more.

You start replaying the last few weeks, looking for the moment it changed. Maybe it was something you said. Maybe it was something at school you never heard about. You didn't do anything wrong. Something shifted in him, and it wasn't aimed at you.

That gap between what he used to say and what he says now is the part that actually hurts. It's also more common, and less about you, than it feels right now.

What's Really Behind the One-Word Answers

A lot of what looks like shutting you out is actually a mask. Not a costume mask. The kind a person puts on so nobody sees what's really going on underneath. One boy in Real Talk describes exactly this. Everyone thought he had it together. He was popular, quick-witted, good at everything he tried. He smiled constantly, but it was starting to feel like a costume. After every speech he gave or song he played, he'd replay it in his head, sure he'd sounded weird. Nobody around him could see any of that. They only saw the mask.

Your son may be running a smaller version of the same act. Acting like nothing bothers him. Staying busy so nobody asks questions. Keeping every conversation light so nobody digs deeper. None of these are lies exactly. They're just easier than saying the true thing out loud, and the longer any of us wears a mask, the harder it gets to remember what's actually underneath it.

Why Honesty Feels Riskier Than Silence

Real Talk draws a useful line here. Honesty isn't unloading everything you feel. It's naming what's actually going on before it grows into something bigger. Saying "I feel left out." Asking "are we okay?" instead of guessing what's wrong.

That's a hard skill even for adults, let alone a fourteen-year-old. One story in the book follows a boy named Jaden, who snapped at his little sister after she accidentally broke his gaming headset. He yelled. He called her a name he regretted the second it left his mouth. But the headset wasn't really what he was mad about. Underneath it was a bad grade he hadn't told anyone about, an assignment he'd missed, and a lunch table where nobody had saved him a seat that day. The headset was just where all of it finally came out.

Your son's silence often works the same way that outburst did for Jaden. The one-word answer usually isn't about the question you asked him. It's whatever he hasn't said yet, sitting underneath it, waiting for somewhere to go.

What Actually Opens the Door

Face to face, with him standing there waiting for a response, feels like an interview. Side by side works better. In the car, eyes on the road, nowhere he has to be for ten minutes, most parents of teenage boys will tell you that's where the real conversations happen. Almost never at the table. Almost always in the car.

The question matters too. "How was your day?" asks for a full report with no warning, and that's a lot to hand someone who's still deciding what's safe to say. A narrower question is easier to answer honestly. Try, "What was the worst part of your day? I'll go first." Or when something feels different between you, name it instead of guessing: "Are we okay? I felt a little off between us and wanted to check." That one move, asking instead of assuming, shows up twice in Real Talk.

Jaden's own turnaround started with something just as small. He didn't fix everything in one conversation. The next morning, he knocked on his sister's door, said he was sorry, and meant it. Then he told his parents the truth about the day he'd actually had, not as an excuse, but as a confession. It cost him something to say that out loud. That was the point, and it's the same size step available to your son, whenever he's ready to take it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my teenage son only give one-word answers?
Around age 12 to 14, a direct question starts to feel like a test he might fail, so a short, safe answer feels less risky than a longer one that could come out wrong. It's rarely about not caring. It's more often about not having the words ready yet.

Is it normal for a teenage boy to stop talking to his parents?
Yes, pulling inward at this age is common and usually isn't a sign of anything serious on its own. It's worth a closer look if it comes with pulling away from things he used to enjoy, changes in sleep or appetite, or a flatness that doesn't lift.

How do I get my teenage son to open up?
Side by side conversations, like in the car, tend to work better than face to face ones, because there's no pressure to perform and nowhere he has to be for a few minutes. Narrow, specific questions also get better answers than broad ones like "how was your day."

What should I say instead of "how was your day?"
Try something specific, like "what was the worst part of your day, I'll go first," which gives him a smaller target and lets you go first. If something feels off between you, ask directly rather than guessing: "are we okay?"
