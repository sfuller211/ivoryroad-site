He still goes to church. You don't have to drag him. He walks in, finds a seat in the back, and pulls his hood up.

He doesn't argue about God. He doesn't say much about God at all. When faith comes up at home, he shrugs or changes the subject. During the prayer, you're not sure he's praying.

You've wondered what's going on in there. Maybe you've wondered if he believes any of it. Or if he believes it and just doesn't think it's for him.

The Real Talk Parent Guide says many teens wonder exactly that.

Struggling With Faith Isn't Always About Whether God Is Real

When a teen son seems far from faith, it's natural to assume he has doubts about God. Sometimes that's true. Real Talk describes another kind of struggle that is easy to miss.

In the book, there's a boy named Noah. He came to youth group most weeks. Hood up. Earbuds in. Back row, head down. He wasn't disrespectful. He just didn't seem to expect anything there to be for him.

Noah believed in God. He just didn't believe God believed much in him.

He thought faith was for the polished kids. The ones who knew the worship songs and shared their testimonies. Nobody had pushed him out. He had already counted himself out in his own mind.

The Parent Guide says this can look like a boy who doesn't care. He sits in the back. He keeps his hood up. He brushes off questions about faith. Underneath, he may be asking something he would never say out loud: Does God really love me like this?

That's a different question from "Is God real?" It needs a different kind of answer.

Why Teen Boys Start Wondering Where They Stand With God

Real Talk opens with the author's own story. He grew up in church. He knew when to raise his hands and when to bow his head. He could win the verse memorization games. And at night, alone, he wondered if God had already started giving up on him.

The message he picked up, though no one said it out loud, was this: God loves the good version of me. The cleaned-up one. The one who tries hard. The messy version, he was sure, disappointed God.

Many boys carry some version of that. They mess up again, zone out during prayer, lose their temper, forget to read their Bible. Each time, they may picture God with His arms crossed. After a while it can feel easier to sit in the back than to keep trying to be good enough.

The Parent Guide adds something parents need to hear. This is the age when young men start taking faith on as their own, not just their family's. That means questions. Am I enough? Does God see me? Have I messed up too much? Doubt is part of that. The Parent Guide says questioning doesn't disqualify him. It's often a step toward deeper faith.

If you've read When Your Son Wonders What God Actually Thinks About Him, you'll recognize the shame side of this. This post is about the other side: what you can say, and what Scripture says, when he isn't sure God wants him.

What God Says, and How a Parent Can Help Him Hear It

The book answers Noah's question the way it answers every boy's. Not with a pep talk. With what God has already said.

1 John 3:1 says the Father has lavished His love on us, "that we should be called children of God." Then the verse adds that this is what we are. Not distant followers. Children.

Psalm 34:18 says, "The Lord is close to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit." When your son feels weak, God doesn't step back. He moves closer.

Romans 8:1 says, "Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." He is not being followed around by a scorecard.

For Noah, the turn came on an ordinary Wednesday night. The leader handed out index cards with one question: "What do you think God sees when He looks at you?" Noah's mind filled with words like lazy, distracted, and failure. Then the leader read Ephesians 2:10, a verse that calls us God's masterpiece. That one word stayed with him. He wrote on his card, "God doesn't just see my mess. He sees His masterpiece in progress." That night he taped it to the corner of his mirror. Not because everything had changed. Because he was starting to believe what God had already said.

You can help your son get there, slowly. The Parent Guide offers a few small ways.

Let him know his questions don't disqualify him. Say it plainly, once, and don't turn it into a talk.

Share your own story. Tell him about a time you wondered if God was disappointed in you, and how you came to see His grace. Then invite him to share one of his, if he wants to.

Leave him a short note where he'll find it. Tell him you see him, you're proud of him, and God isn't letting go of him.

Catch him in an ordinary moment, after practice or grabbing a snack, and name something true about his character.

He may shrug. He may roll his eyes. The Parent Guide is honest about that. These conversations still aren't wasted. You're planting seeds, and God will water them in His timing.

If you see more than a faith question, like he seems low most days for a few weeks, or he's pulling back from the things he used to enjoy, start with him. Pick a calm moment, tell him what you've noticed, and listen more than you talk.

A boy in the back row is still in the room. Jeremiah 31:3 says, "I have loved you with an everlasting love; I have drawn you with unfailing kindness." That kindness doesn't run out on the days he isn't sure.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do if my teen son is struggling with faith?
Start by listening for the real question. Some boys question whether God is real. Others believe in God and aren't sure God wants them. Share your own doubts, keep conversations short, and let him know that questions don't push him out of faith.

Is it normal for a Christian teenager to doubt God?
Yes. The teen years are when many young men start owning their faith instead of borrowing their family's. Questions are part of that. In Real Talk's Parent Guide, doubt is described as often being a step toward deeper faith, not away from it.

Why does my son sit in the back at church and avoid talking about God?
It can look like he doesn't care. Often it's closer to self-doubt. He may think faith is for the kids who have it together, and assume God is disappointed in him. The Parent Guide says these aren't signs of rebellion. They're a chance to remind him that God's love is unshakable.

What Bible verses can I share with a teen who feels far from God?
Real Talk points to 1 John 3:1, Psalm 34:18, Jeremiah 31:3, and Romans 8:1. Together they say he is God's child, God draws near when he's hurting, God's love doesn't run out, and there is no condemnation for those in Christ. Share one at a time, simply, at breakfast or bedtime.
