He's the funny one. At school, at practice, at your table. His friends text him first. When a room needs to loosen up, he's the one who does it.

You're proud of that. You've also noticed something you haven't said out loud. You can't remember the last time he told you how he actually was.

You ask, and he gives you a joke. You try again, and he gives you a better one. It's good company. It's also a very good way to keep a conversation from going anywhere.

Real Talk tells a story about a boy like this, and it shows what can be going on while everyone is laughing.

Why the Funny One Rarely Gets Asked

When a boy is easy to be around, people stop checking on him. Nothing looks wrong, so nobody asks.

In Real Talk, there's a star player everyone likes. Loud laugh, quick with a joke, the kind of guy you'd assume has it figured out. One afternoon after practice, everyone else cleared out. He stayed behind with the coach. His voice was low when he asked his question. He wanted to know if he was even good enough to stay on the team.

The player who overheard it froze. He couldn't believe what he was hearing.

That's the part parents rarely see. At home, the same boy may turn serious moments into jokes. He may say "I don't care" about something that clearly matters. Sometimes it goes the other way. He does everything right and stays busy, and nobody notices the pressure he's under.

The Parent Guide says these moments may not mean he's hiding something dark. They do point to a fear of being found out.

It helps to know how ordinary this is. The Parent Guide calls image management a survival skill at school. Social media, friends, and the culture around him all push a boy to look stronger and cooler than he feels. Pretending is normal. It's also tiring, and it can leave him feeling alone in a room full of people who like him.

What He's Afraid Will Happen If He Stops

A mask feels like protection at first. It keeps him from being embarrassed or judged. Over time, it starts to shape him. He wears it so often that he starts to believe it's who he is.

Under that is a second fear, and boys almost never say it. If he isn't the funny one, what's left? Will people still trust him if they see the cracks?

His phone makes it worse. He scrolls past guys who look like they have it all together, and he decides he's the only one who doesn't. Real Talk says what he needs to hear: you're not. Everyone fakes it sometimes, even the confident ones.

The chapter adds one more thing. The longer he keeps it up, the harder it gets to remember what was real to begin with. That may be where the tiredness comes from, the kind you can see even when he's laughing.

The Parent Guide has a line for this too. Masks may impress people, but they don't build trust. A boy who is only ever admired may wonder whether people would still trust him if the act stopped.

What Changes When Home Isn't a Stage

You can't take the mask off for him. You can make home a place where he doesn't need it. The Parent Guide puts it this way: make home a place where he doesn't have to be "on" to be accepted.

It starts small. When he says something honest, even half a sentence, thank him for it. Then let that be the whole conversation. Skip the fix.

Go first. Tell him one way you've been tempted to fake it at work or with friends. Keep it short, and don't turn it into a lesson. If you want to say something about the jokes, say it kindly and in private, never in front of his friends. "I like how you make people laugh. You don't have to be on with me." Then stop talking.

The chapter also has a question worth borrowing. At a small group, a leader asked, "What's one thing you've been pretending isn't a big deal, but it actually is?" The room went quiet, and the quiet didn't feel threatening. No one rushed anyone. It felt like a pause waiting for someone to go first.

A boy named Micah said four words: "I'm tired of pretending." Another guy whispered, "Me too." Nobody laughed, and nothing fell apart.

You don't need a small group. You need the pause. Ask once, at an ordinary time, and let the silence sit. He may say nothing. That's allowed. Rest helps too. The Parent Guide suggests a walk, a few minutes of sitting in silence, or praying together, so he can feel he doesn't have to perform.

There's a verse the Parent Guide offers for this. 1 Samuel 16:7 says, "People look at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart." A boy who spends his energy managing what people see may hear that as relief. Nobody is grading the performance. Matthew 11:28 says it another way: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest."

Jokes are not a warning sign by themselves. If he's also pulling back from things he used to enjoy, or seems low most days for a few weeks, start with him. Pick a calm moment, tell him what you've noticed, and don't push for answers.

Funny can stay. It's a gift. What you're working toward is a home where it isn't the only thing he has to offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a problem if my teenage son jokes instead of talking about serious things?
Not by itself. Humor is often a real strength. It's worth a closer look when jokes are all you ever get, or when they show up the moment a subject turns serious. Then the joke may be protecting him from being seen.

Why does my teenage son hide his feelings when he seems happy?
Many boys are afraid someone will see through the act. They worry people will lose respect or trust if they see the cracks. Being the funny one or the together one feels safer than saying what's going on. Over time it can wear him out.

How can I get my teenage son to be honest about how he feels?
Keep it small. Thank him for any honest moment, even a short one. Share one true thing about your own week first. Ask one question, then let it sit. A big talk usually gets another joke.

What does the Bible say about hiding who we really are?
1 Samuel 16:7 says the Lord looks at the heart, not the outward appearance. 2 Corinthians 12:9 says, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." In Real Talk, God isn't asking your son to keep up the act. He sees past it and stays.
