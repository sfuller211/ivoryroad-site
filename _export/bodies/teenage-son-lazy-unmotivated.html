He gets the assignment on a Monday. Three weeks to do it. Plenty of time.

For two and a half weeks, nothing. Then the light is still on at midnight the night before, and it's finished by one.

Forty minutes. That's how long it took him.

The next afternoon you bring it up. You tell him he could have done so much better. You tell him he's wasting what he's got. You ask him why he does this to himself, and you mean it as a real question.

He shrugs. Goes to his room.

And the word shows up, even if you never say it out loud. Lazy. He's just lazy.

I want to hand you a different word. Not because lazy is unkind, though it is. Because it's usually wrong, and being wrong about this is why next month goes exactly the same way.

What He's Actually Doing While He's Not Doing It

Go back to those three weeks and watch him. He is not relaxed.

He knows it's there. He thinks about it at strange hours. He opens the laptop and closes it. He decides he'll start after dinner, then after this one episode, then tomorrow, definitely tomorrow. At one point he cleans his room, which he has never once done without being asked.

That is not what not caring looks like. Not caring is easy. Not caring sleeps fine.

What you are watching is a boy circling something.

There is a real difference between a boy who has no motivation and a boy who has too much riding on it. From the outside the two look identical. Both of them are sitting there not working. But one of them is empty, and the other one is full of something he can't set down.

Most of the time, it's the second one.

And the more it matters to him, the longer he circles. That's the part that makes no sense from the kitchen. The stuff he doesn't care about, he knocks out in an evening. The stuff that counts is the stuff that sits there for three weeks.

If you want to know which boy you've got, watch what he avoids. It is almost never random.

Why Trying Feels Riskier Than Not Trying

Here is the math he is doing without knowing he's doing it.

If he doesn't really try and it comes back bad, the grade means nothing. He didn't try. It proves nothing about him.

If he tries hard, actually hard, gives it everything he has, and it still comes back bad, now he knows something about himself he did not want to know.

So he protects the not-knowing.

Forty minutes the night before is a shield. It keeps the question open. He gets to tell himself he would have done better if he'd started sooner, and as long as he never starts sooner, he never has to find out whether that's true.

In Real Talk I called this one of the lies fear tells a boy: if I can't do it perfectly, it's not worth trying.

Chapter 10 tells the story of a boy named Liam. He stayed away from anything that might make him look foolish. Then he got assigned a presentation he had to give alone, in front of the class. He thought seriously about faking sick.

The night before, his older sister said something he wasn't expecting. "You don't have to do it perfectly. You just have to do it, even if you're scared." She wrote out Isaiah 41:10 on a sticky note and handed it to him.

His knees wobbled. His mouth went dry. He stumbled, and somewhere in the middle he forgot a line. He kept going anyway.

He didn't give a good presentation. He gave it. That was the win. He had spent years getting out of things like that, and this time he didn't, and now he knows he can stand up there and live through it. The grade was beside the point.

Your son hasn't had that yet. He's still getting out of things, and every time he does, the next one gets a little bigger.

What Changes When You Stop Calling It Laziness

You still have a son who has to do the work. Nothing here gets him out of that, and you are not wrong to expect it.

But the word you reach for decides what you say next. And what you say next decides whether he tells you anything at all.

Lazy is a verdict. It tells him you already worked out what's wrong with him. There's nothing left for him to add, so he doesn't add anything. He shrugs, goes to his room, and you are both exactly where you started.

The other word is stuck. Stuck has a question attached to it.

So try these instead, and say them at a normal moment, not at midnight.

Instead of "is it done yet," try "what's the first thing you'd have to do?" Finishing is the part that feels impossible to him. One small piece is something he can picture doing tonight.

Instead of "why do you always wait," try "what makes starting hard?" The first sounds like an accusation. The second is an actual question, and he can tell the difference.

And when he tells you he'll get to it when he's ready, tell him the truth. "Nobody feels ready. You start, and the ready catches up." Say it once, in a normal voice, and let it sit there.

Then notice when he starts, not just when he finishes. "I saw you got going on that" is enough.

Because he is watching which one you care about. Right now he is fairly sure the answer is the grade.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my teenage son so lazy and unmotivated?
In most cases he isn't. What looks like no motivation is usually avoidance, and avoidance is usually fear of what trying might prove about him. A boy who truly doesn't care is relaxed about it. A boy who stalls, cleans his room, and does the work at midnight is not relaxed. He is protecting himself from finding out.

How do I motivate my teenage son without nagging?
Stop asking him to finish it and ask him what the first step is. Finishing is the part that feels impossible, so he refuses to start at all. One small piece is something he can picture doing tonight. Then say something when he starts, not only when he finishes.

Is my teenage son lazy or is something else going on?
Worth taking seriously. Avoidance around one hard task is normal. A change that covers everything is different. If he has pulled back from things he used to enjoy, if sleep or appetite has shifted, if it has run for weeks rather than days, that is worth a conversation with your doctor or a counselor. You are not overreacting by asking.

What should I say when my son puts everything off until the last minute?
Wait until it isn't an emergency, because nobody hears anything the night before. Then, at a neutral moment, ask what makes starting hard. Not why he does this. What makes starting hard. One is an accusation and one is a question, and he knows which he's being handed.
