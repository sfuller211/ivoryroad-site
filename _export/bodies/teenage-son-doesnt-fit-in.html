There was a stretch when he talked about it all the time. At dinner, in the car, any time you asked about his day. Maybe it was a band he wanted to start, or a game he'd been building for weeks, or a sport nobody else at his school cares about. You could hardly get him to stop.

Then one week, he did stop.

You asked how it was going, and he said, "It's fine." You asked if he was still working on it, and he shrugged. Whatever it is, it hasn't gone anywhere. It's still on his desk, or in his closet, or saved on his phone. He just doesn't bring it up anymore.

You may never find out what happened. Someone laughed, or rolled their eyes. It was small enough that he'd never call it a big deal, and big enough that he decided to keep that part of himself quiet.

There's a story in Real Talk about a boy named Jordan that shows what that moment can feel like from the inside.

What He Decides After Someone Laughs

Jordan was the only one in his class who loved astronomy. While other guys talked about football and sneakers, Jordan talked about constellations and Mars rovers. He wasn't trying to stand out. He just loved space.

At the school science night, he set up his telescope and printed star charts to hand out. Most kids walked past. Some laughed. One said, loud enough for everyone to hear, "Who brings a space cannon to school night?"

Jordan laughed it off. But it stung. He almost didn't sign up for the next one. Maybe he'd pick a normal topic, something safe and invisible.

That's the moment to pay attention to. He wasn't deciding whether space was interesting. He was deciding whether it was worth the cost.

Your son may be doing the same math. Most boys don't say it out loud. They just get quieter about the thing they love, and the quiet looks like a mood.

What Is Going On Under the Quiet

It's easy to read this as a problem with a hobby. It's really a problem with identity. The Parent Guide puts it this way: these moments "aren't about fashion, hobbies, or friend groups." Under the telescope is a bigger question. If people laugh at what I love, am I still worth keeping as I am?

At his age, being the same as everyone else looks like the safe answer. Real Talk opens this chapter with two boys who own the exact same backpack. Same brand, same color, same zipper pulls. They swap bags by accident and don't notice until they're halfway home. Then someone jokes, "Wouldn't life be easier if we were all the same?"

It would be easier. That's the pull. If he's the same as everyone else, nobody laughs.

So he starts to edit himself. At home, it can look like pulling back. He plays down what he's good at. He makes jokes at his own expense about what sets him apart. He seems like a slightly different person depending on who he's with.

He isn't being fake. He's testing what is safe.

But hiding has a price, and the chapter names it. When he spends his energy hiding what makes him different, he doesn't disappear. He shrinks into someone he was never supposed to be.

What Changed for Jordan, and What It Means for You

Jordan didn't shrink. Something in him said, "Show up anyway," and he went back.

This time one kid walked up and said, "Hey, that was awesome last time. Can you show me how to use it?"

Jordan didn't become popular. No party, no spotlight. What changed was smaller. He stopped needing everyone to understand him. He just needed to stop hiding. And his willingness to be different gave someone else permission to be curious.

Look at how little it took. One person. Not a crowd.

Your son may be waiting for that one person, and you can be the one. You can't change the room he walks into. But you can make sure home is the one place where the thing he loves never gets a laugh.

The Parent Guide gives a simple way to start. Ask him one question: "When have you felt most different from everyone else?" Then tell him about a time you did. Two or three sentences from your own school years is enough. Don't turn it into a lesson.

Later, when it's quiet, you can name one difference you admire in him. Make it something you have actually seen, like how long he'll stay with a hard problem or how he notices what other people miss. Say it once, plainly, and let it be.

Under all of it is something worth saying to him more than once. Zephaniah 3:17 says God will "rejoice over you with singing." Not once he fits in. Right now.

If he seems to be pulling away from everyone, not just from one interest, and it lasts for weeks, talk with his doctor, a school counselor, or a trusted pastor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my teenage son feel like he doesn't fit in?
Many teen boys feel out of step with their peers. It might be the way he dresses, the way he thinks, or what he cares about. It doesn't mean something is wrong with him. During these years, boys compare themselves closely to the guys who seem to fit the mold, and differences start to feel like flaws.

Is it normal for a teen boy to stop talking about what he loves?
It's very common. A laugh or a joke from a classmate can be enough. He decides the interest isn't worth the embarrassment, so he keeps it private. The interest is usually still alive. He's just protecting it.

How can I help my teenage son feel okay about being different?
Keep it small. Name one specific thing you admire in how he's wired, based on something you have actually seen. Share a time you felt out of step at his age. Then leave room for him to respond, or not. A big speech can make a self-conscious boy more self-conscious.

Does God care that my son feels different?
Yes. Job 10:8 says, "Your hands shaped me and made me." Zephaniah 3:17 says God delights in him and rejoices over him with singing. In Real Talk, being different isn't treated as a flaw to fix. It's treated as part of how God made him.
