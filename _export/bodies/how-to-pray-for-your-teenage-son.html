You used to hear him. A quick "thank you, God" before dinner. A mumbled prayer at bedtime that you half listened to from the hallway.

Now there's nothing. Maybe he asked God for one thing that really mattered and it didn't happen. Maybe there was no moment at all, just a slow fade. Either way, he hasn't said a word about God in months.

And you don't know what that means. Did he stop believing? Or did he just stop talking about it in front of you? You're not sure whether to bring it up. You're not sure what to pray while you wait.

That second question is the one you can do something about today.

Why He Went Quiet About Prayer

Most teenage boys who go quiet about prayer haven't walked away from God. They've walked away from doing it where anyone can hear. We wrote about that in more depth in Teen Boys and Prayer: Why They Go Silent, but here is the short version.

Somewhere he picked up the idea that prayer has a right way to sound. Long sentences. The right words in the right order. A youth pastor who prays beautifully out loud. A grandparent whose prayers sound like they were written in advance. He compared himself to that, decided he couldn't do it, and quietly opted out.

He isn't grading his prayers against God. He's grading them against the best prayer he's ever heard someone else say. Nobody wins that comparison, including you.

Real Talk opens its prayer chapter with a young man who felt exactly this way. He thought prayer belonged to people with polished spiritual words. Then a friend was hurting, and all he could get out was, "God, help him." Three words. That was the whole prayer, and it changed how he understood prayer from then on.

That's the hopeful part. This is a confidence problem more than a faith problem, and confidence is something you can help build.

What to Pray for Your Teenage Son When You Can't Read Him

You don't need information you don't have. You only need to be honest about what you can see.

If you don't know what's going on with him, say that. "God, I don't know what's happening with him right now. Help me notice what he isn't saying."

If you think he's holding something, hand it over. "God, whatever he's carrying, You already know it. Meet him there before I do."

If you feel the urge to pull answers out of him, pray about the urge. "God, give me the patience to wait for him."

And if he's gone quiet about God, pray for the quiet itself. "God, let him know You're still there, even in the months he doesn't mention You."

None of those need you to know what's wrong. They only need you to admit that you don't.

Keep them short. Real Talk describes prayer as being "more like a text to someone who's already in the room." It doesn't have to be long. It just has to be real. That's as true for you as it is for him. A tired parent at eleven at night doesn't need a better prayer. One honest sentence counts.

Pray Out Loud. On Purpose.

The fastest way to lower the bar for him is to lower it for yourself first, where he can see it.

Skip the polished dinner-table prayer for a week. Say something short and plain instead. "God, thanks for today. Help us tomorrow." Let it be a little awkward. Let it trail off.

He isn't learning prayer from what you tell him about it. He's learning it from what he watches you do. A polished prayer, every time, teaches him that prayer is a skill he doesn't have. An unfinished one said out loud shows him this is something regular people do.

The same goes for how you bring it up. "Have you been praying about it?" sounds like a check on his homework. Try this instead: "I still pray about that one. I don't always know what to say either."

That one sentence does two things at once. It gives him permission, and it shows him what an honest prayer life sounds like from someone he knows.

When He Tells You Prayer Didn't Work

He might say it someday. He prayed for something that mattered, nothing happened, so why bother.

Don't argue him out of the disappointment. It's real. Rushing past it tells him the feeling was the problem.

Chapter 12 of Real Talk tells the story of Marcus, who didn't grow up in a family that prayed. God felt like a distant idea. Then his mom got sick. Sitting in the hospital, scared and not knowing what to say to anyone, he whispered, "God... please help her."

No miracle moment. No instant relief. But something in his chest shifted, just enough to take one deep breath. And he kept going, one line at a time. "Thank You." "Be with her." "Help me not fall apart." The more he whispered, the less weird it felt.

That's what most prayer looks like. Not a switch flipping. Something loosening, slowly, over time.

You can tell him that plainly. Prayer isn't a vending machine. Sometimes what changes is him, not the situation, and that still counts.

And if you've had a prayer go unanswered, tell him about it. Hearing that you kept talking to God anyway may do more than any explanation you could give.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I pray for my teenage son?
Keep it short and honest. You don't need to know what's going on with him to pray for him. "God, I don't know what he's carrying, but You do" is a real prayer. One sentence prayed often does more than a long prayer prayed once.

What should I do if my teenage son stopped praying?
Don't push or quiz him about it. Most boys who stop praying out loud haven't stopped believing. They feel like they can't do it right. Pray short, plain prayers in front of him so he sees that his kind of prayer counts.

How do I teach my teenage son to pray without pressure?
Show him instead of teaching him. Let him hear you pray a short, imperfect prayer at dinner or in the car. Don't put him on the spot to pray out loud in front of others until he's ready.

What do I say when my son says prayer doesn't work?
Start by agreeing that it hurts. Don't rush to fix the feeling. Then tell him what you've found to be true: prayer doesn't always change the situation, but it often changes the person praying. If you've been there yourself, tell him.
